In the second pillar of every engagement I run, we do something that sounds trivial and turns out to be the most uncomfortable part of the process: we go back through the client's career and write down what actually happened, with numbers.

It takes about three weeks. Roughly every client finds something they had forgotten was impressive. And almost every client discovers they have been systematically rounding their own record downward.

## Three ways capable people erase their own evidence

**Attribution dilution.** "We grew the division from $30M to $95M." Who is we? In the client's head, saying "I" felt like theft, because eleven other people worked on it. The result is a sentence that credits nobody and therefore persuades nobody. The honest version is more specific, not less: "I led the division through a $30M to $95M expansion across four years, running a team of eleven."

**Temporal flattening.** Achievements get described in the tense of a résumé, which strips out the difficulty. "Turned around underperforming region" is a bullet. "Took a region that had missed target for seven consecutive quarters and had it profitable in five" is evidence, because it contains the resistance.

**Number avoidance.** This is the most common and the most costly. Senior operators frequently refuse to state figures because the figures are approximate, commercially sensitive, or feel boastful. So they substitute adjectives. Adjectives are unfalsifiable, which means they are also unbelievable. "Significant improvement in margin" carries less weight than "EBITDA from 11 to 34 percent," and the second one is not less professional — it is more.

## The retrieval process

I use four passes, and I do them in this order because each one surfaces material the next one needs.

**Pass one: the chronology.** Every role, every project, every mandate, in order, with dates. No commentary. Most people can produce this in an hour and most people are surprised by its length.

**Pass two: the state change.** For each entry, two columns: what was true when you arrived, what was true when you left. This is where forgotten achievements reappear, because the contrast is what makes them visible. If you cannot fill in the "before" column, you have found a gap worth investigating rather than an item to delete.

**Pass three: quantification.** Attach a number to every state change, even where the number is approximate or has to be expressed as a range. Approximate numbers stated with a stated margin are far more credible than adjectives. "Somewhere between forty and fifty percent, and I can show you the reporting" is completely acceptable.

**Pass four: verification.** For your five strongest items, identify a person who could confirm it and a document that records it. This pass has a secondary benefit: it tells you which claims you can put in public without anxiety, which is usually what has been holding you back from making them at all.

## What this is actually for

The output is not a résumé. It is a library you draw from constantly and never rebuild: the specific example for a sales conversation, the credential line for a speaker introduction, the case narrative for a proposal, the story that makes a keynote land.

Clients report the same unexpected effect, which is worth naming. Having your evidence organised changes how you behave in rooms. Not because you recite it — you rarely do — but because the vague anxiety about whether you have earned your position disappears when the answer is written down and verifiable.

That is not a branding outcome. It is a psychological one, and it tends to show up in the first month, well before anything external changes.
