There is a sentence I hear in roughly nine out of ten first calls. It arrives in slightly different words, but the structure is always identical:

> "I've been doing this for eighteen years. I'm genuinely one of the best people in the country at it. And I have no idea why the work isn't coming to me."

The answer is almost never capability. It is that expertise and position are different assets, and only one of them is legible from the outside.

## Expertise is internal. Position is comparative.

Expertise is a property of you. It accumulates privately, through repetition, failure and pattern recognition. Nobody can observe it directly. What people can observe are proxies: how you speak, what you have published, who vouches for you, what you are known for.

A position is different in kind. A position is a claim about where you sit relative to alternatives — including the alternative of doing nothing. It is not a description of your skill; it is an instruction about when someone should call you rather than somebody else.

The test is uncomfortable and simple. Ask three people who know your work to state, in one sentence, what you specifically do and who for. If the three sentences differ materially, you do not have a position. You have a reputation for competence, which is pleasant and commercially inert.

## Why accurate descriptions fail

Most senior operators describe themselves accurately. That is exactly the problem.

Consider two statements about the same person:

1. "I help mid-market manufacturing businesses improve operational efficiency across supply chain, procurement and production planning."
2. "I fix the six-week lead time that is quietly costing you your best customers."

The first is more accurate. It covers more of what he actually does, and he was reluctant to abandon it for that reason. The second is a position: narrower, more falsifiable, and it names a situation the buyer recognises in themselves.

Accuracy optimises for completeness. Positions optimise for recall. When someone in a meeting says "we have a lead time problem," you want to be the name that surfaces automatically. Nobody's brain surfaces a comprehensive service list.

## The cost of narrowing, honestly stated

Choosing a position costs you something real, and advisors who pretend otherwise are lying to you.

You will lose enquiries. The manufacturing consultant above stopped receiving procurement-only briefs, which had been about a fifth of his pipeline. He gained something better: he became the default recommendation inside a specific, painful, expensive situation, and the enquiries that arrived were larger and less price-sensitive.

That trade is only worth making deliberately. The mistake is not narrowing — the mistake is narrowing accidentally, which is what happens when you refuse to choose and let the market decide what you are for based on whichever project happened to be most recent.

## What to do about it this month

Three exercises, in order. They are unglamorous and they work.

**One: collect your own language.** Go through six months of your sent email and find the sentences where you explain what you do to someone who genuinely does not know. Not your website copy — your actual writing under mild pressure. The position is usually already in there, said casually, and then never repeated in public.

**Two: name the situation, not the service.** Write down the specific circumstance a client is in the week before they contact you. What broke? What did somebody say in a meeting? Your position lives in that circumstance, because that is the moment of recall.

**Three: write the sentence that costs you something.** If your positioning statement would be comfortable for four of your competitors to also use, it is a description. Rewrite it until at least one type of client would read it and correctly conclude that you are not for them.

That last one is the whole discipline, really. A position you could lose work because of is the only kind that wins work reliably.
