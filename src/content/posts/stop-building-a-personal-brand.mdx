The phrase "personal brand" has become almost useless, and not because it is vulgar. It is useless because it describes an outcome as though it were an activity. Nobody can do personal branding, in the same way that nobody can do reputation.

What you can do is build assets that produce reputation as a side effect. The highest-return one available to a senior operator is a named method.

## Why a method beats a following

A following is an asset you rent. It sits on infrastructure you do not control, it decays without continuous input, and it correlates weakly with commercial outcomes at the executive level. I have clients with 400,000 followers and no inbound pipeline, and clients with 4,000 followers whose calendars are full six months out.

A method behaves differently in three ways that matter.

**It is portable.** Someone can describe your framework in a meeting you are not in. This is the actual mechanism by which senior work arrives — not discovery, but referral with substance attached. "You should talk to Priya" is weak. "She has a four-pillar thing, the second pillar is basically an evidence audit, that is exactly your problem" is a qualified referral that arrives pre-sold.

**It compounds.** Every time you use a named framework, it accumulates association. The fifth use is more valuable than the first, and the fiftieth is more valuable still. Follower counts do not work this way; posts have a half-life measured in hours.

**It survives you being unavailable.** Your method can be in a book, a deck your client's team circulates, or a slide in someone else's presentation. It works while you are asleep, on holiday, or fully booked.

## What makes a method credible rather than cosmetic

The industry produces a great deal of framework theatre: five alliterative words arranged in a circle, invented to look proprietary. Informed audiences detect this instantly, and the damage is worse than having no framework at all.

Four tests separate real methods from decoration.

**It has to be falsifiable.** A real method asserts that doing things in a particular order produces better outcomes than doing them in another order. That claim can be argued with. "Clarity, Confidence, Consistency, Community" cannot be argued with, which is why it persuades nobody who has been in the industry more than a decade.

**It has to be derived from your actual practice.** Write down the last six engagements and find what you did in all six. That is your method, and it is discovered rather than designed. If your framework contains a step you have never actually performed, remove it.

**It has to be granular enough to be useful.** Each component needs a deliverable a client could hold. "Alignment" is a mood. "A positioning statement, a named enemy and three defensible claims" is a deliverable.

**It has to explain failure.** This is the strongest credibility signal and the one most frameworks lack. A real method predicts what goes wrong when it is not followed. Mine claims that attempting distribution before positioning produces a louder version of an unclear message — which is a specific, checkable prediction, and it is why I can tell in ten minutes on a call why a client's previous branding investment did not work.

## Naming it without embarrassing yourself

Two failure modes: too clever and too generic. Too clever ages badly and makes you sound like you are selling something. Too generic fails to attach.

The reliable approach is plain language describing the mechanism, not the outcome. Name what happens, not what the client feels afterward. And say it out loud in a sentence you would actually use with a peer — if you would be embarrassed saying it to a chief executive over lunch, it is wrong, regardless of how it looks on a slide.

Then use it for a year without changing it. The temptation to keep refining the name is enormous and always counterproductive. A mediocre name used consistently for twelve months outperforms an excellent name that changes every quarter, because the entire value is in accumulated association.

That is the trade at the centre of all of this. Attention is available immediately and depreciates. Standing takes longer than anyone wants it to and compounds indefinitely. Choose deliberately, and be honest with yourself about which one you are actually buying when you pay for it.
